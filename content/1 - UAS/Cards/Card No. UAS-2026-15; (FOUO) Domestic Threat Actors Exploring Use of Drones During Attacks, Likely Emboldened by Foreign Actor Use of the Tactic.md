**(U//FOUO) Domestic Actors Drawing Inspiration from Drone Tactics in the Russo-Ukrainian War, Several Instances of Drone Use During Planned Attacks in the US

(U//FOUO) ISB assesses that domestic threat actors are likely exploring the use of drones during future attacks, driven in part by successful employment of drones in armed conflict, specifically the ongoing Russo-Ukrainian War. The advanced expertise and tactics seen in the conflict may act as guidance to domestic actors considering employing drones for attacks. Open sources indicate that individuals in online communities associated with domestic violent extremist ideologies have discussed incorporating drones in attacks, and several domestic threat actors have successfully used or plotted to use drones during attacks in the United States.
- (U) As recently as April 20, 2026, an individual advocated for the use of a weaponized drone against a well-known CEO of a tech company, noting “[named CEO] has a home. You can visit him with a drone from temu and some explosives. Its literally that easy.”1 Additionally, in June 2025, several unidentified 4chanUSPER users discussed how to construct drones effectively for future assassination attempts while referencing the recent murders of two US politicians. One user stated, “Cheap drone used in [Ukraine]. Strap a homemade explosive to it with a remote detonation and you can kill any politician without being caught… they are so easy to use and eliminate these [expletives].”
- (U) From August 2024 to May 2025, a member of a popular white supremacist accelerationist SubstackUSPER suggested to their followers that drone tactics in Ukraine are the “future” and that “militants… who wish to take an active part in the struggle” should acquire drones. The author of this post claims to be a US military veteran fighting in Ukraine, as well as a former member of an identified neo-Nazi violent extremist group.
- (U) On November 2, 2024, the FBI arrested a Tennessee man for plotting to blow up a Nashville electrical substation with an explosive laden drone in furtherance of his white supremacist accelerationism goals. Just two weeks earlier on October 18, 2024, the FBI arrested an Arizona teen for plotting to use an explosive laden drone in an ISIS-inspired attack against a Phoenix Pride event.
- (U) In October 2024, authorities arrested a Maine man for using drones to drop improvised explosive devices on multiple targets to attack or intimidate individuals the suspect was in a local political dispute with.10 On July 13, 2024, the perpetrator of the attempted assassination of President Donald TrumpUSPER in Butler, Pennsylvania used a drone to surveil the rally site where the President was set to speak hours later. This action potentially aided the perpetrator in their attack against the President.

**(U//FOUO) Drone Tactics Used by FTOs and TCOs Likely to Further Inspire Domestic Actors

(U//FOUO) ISB assesses that the successful weaponization and operationalization of drones by foreign terrorist organizations (FTOs) and transnational criminal organizations (TCOs) will further embolden domestic threat actors to adopt these tactics. FTO propaganda frequently highlights drone exploits and encourages ideological adherents to utilize drones in attacks against the West. Separately, several Mexico-based TCOs utilize drones for targeted attacks, criminal operations, and surveillance. FTOs and cartels’ tactics demonstrate the successful use of drones for malicious intent, which may embolden and guide domestic threat actors exploring the tactic.
- (U) As of December 2025, over 469 non-state armed groups globally – including FTOs, militias, and cartels – have employed the use of weaponized drones in the past five years, with 58 groups using them for the first time in 2025. From 2018 through September 2024, drone attacks from non-state actors resulted in approximately 1,300 deaths worldwide. For example, on January 28, 2024, an Iranian-backed militia used an explosive-laden drone to kill three American soldiers and wound approximately 40 more at a military outpost in Jordan.
- (U//FOUO) From June 2024 through June 2025, a pro-ISIS media outlet published a 6-part series on utilizing drones for surveillance and attacks. In the 6th issue of the series, the unit suggests utilizing 3-D printed drop mechanisms comparable to techniques used in the Russo-Ukrainian War.
- (U) Among TCOs, Jalisco Cartel New Generation (CJNG), the Sinaloa Cartel, and the La Nueva Familia Michoacana (LNFM) have been the most prolific drone users. CJNG has maintained a specialized drone operator unit among its forces since 2020. These groups employ weaponized drones against rival criminal factions, federal security forces, and local self-defense groups for aerial bombings, direct kamikaze attacks, and psychological warfare operations.
- (U) Over a nine-month period from May 2023-January 2024, LNFM conducted multiple drone strikes along towns and local communities in Guerrero, Mexico during a feud with a rival criminal group.20 21 22 Additionally, in April 2025, a video appeared online showing CJNG utilizing an explosive one-way kamikaze drone.

**(U//FOUO) Drones Conveying Chemical Agents and WMDs

(U//FOUO) One of the most concerning uses of weaponized drones by extremist groups is the use of drones to drop and disperse chemical agents, a major escalation with probable direct consequences for civilians. The use of toxic gases is classified as a weapon of mass destruction in international law, highlighting the growing level of sophistication in lethal drone weaponization and the potential for more lethal and challenging tactics to proliferate to US-based actors.

(U) In April 2024, CJNG ramped up attacks in the Mexican state of Michoacán, utilizing drones to drop explosive devices filled with chemical substances which local residences stated emitted a toxic gas causing respiratory distress.

**(U//FOUO) Drones Offer Increased Operational Flexibility and Stand-off Capabilities, Making Them Appealing to Threat Actors Across Ideologies

(U//FOUO) ISB assesses that recent technological advancements, which have increased the availability and affordability of drones, have significantly lowered the barrier to entry for threat actors. For a few hundred dollars, anyone can purchase or build a drone, customize it with add-ons like a release mechanism, and outfit it to carry an explosive payload. Threat actors likely find drones attractive as a force-multiplier because they increase operational flexibility and stand-off capability, while reducing threats to the operator during attacks. Interested threat actors can also find open-source training materials and tutorials for operating, building, and modifying drones in online communities, lowering the need for technical understanding for lone actors with limited formal training.
- (U//FOUO) Technological advancements over the last decade have decreased the cost and size of drones while increasing their speed and payload capacity. Popular commercial over-the-counter drones are available for a few hundred dollars and many in this range offer high quality cameras, advanced flight controls, GPS stabilization, and obstacle avoidance features.
- (U//FOUO) The emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) systems also offers new capabilities for drones. Using AI systems, some drones offer autonomous flight paths and features like autopilot that do not require an operator to engage during the attack at all.
- (U//FOUO) In November 2025, an ISIS supporter shared a drone construction guide in a pro-ISIS chat group, and the guide included instructions on how to assemble, calibrate, and fly a drone. A US-based university also published a guide on how to use open source software to build a homemade drone, complete with GPS guidance and capable of carrying a 10 pound payload for 10 minutes, for under $2,000.
- (U//FOUO) Prolific in the ongoing Russo-Ukrainian war, first-person view (FPV) drones now give threat actors greater stand-off capabilities, allowing pilots to use virtual reality goggles to see through the drones’ cameras. This allows a pilot to effectively navigate difficult terrain and attack moving targets. In April 2025, CJNG allegedly used a FPV drone to attack a rival faction in Mexico, providing the first video evidence of a cartel adopting this emerging technology.
