(U) FCC is expanding its restrictions on certain Chinese-origin drones and related devices by targeting nine companies that sell products described as DJI clones, with approvals set to be revoked after a 30-day public comment period.

(U) The article (updated July 28, 2026) reports that the FCC is retroactively revoking existing device authorizations for products from these firms. Once the comment period ends (roughly mid-August, barring a change), the products are expected to land on the FCC’s Covered List. Placement on that list effectively prevents new sales or imports in the US because covered entities cannot obtain the required FCC approvals for consumer electronics. Existing units already owned by consumers should continue to work, and support (including firmware updates) is expected to continue for at least some brands.

(U) Two consumer-facing brands stand out:

- **Skyrover** (linked to SZ Knowact and WaveGo): Markets clones of DJI Mini-series drones (e.g., Skyrover X1 matching the Mini 4 Pro in hardware, camera, gimbal, and app behavior; S1 based on Mini 3).
- **Xtra**: Sells rebranded versions of DJI Osmo cameras (Muse ≈ Pocket 3; Sphra360 ≈ Osmo 360; Edge/Edge Pro ≈ Action 4/5; Atto ≈ Nano). Upcoming Muse 2 / Muse 2 Pro models (clones of the Osmo Pocket 4 / 4P) will not reach the US market; preorder refunds have already begun.

(U) The other companies primarily involve agricultural, enterprise, or industrial drones that the FCC links to DJI technology or design (e.g., Cogito Specta models mirroring Mini 4K and Air 3; Spatial Hover versions of Mavic 3 Enterprise; various Agras-style crop sprayers from Lyno Dynamics, Skyhigh Tech, Fikaxo, and XAG). The FCC’s rationale cites Section 1709 of the National Defense Authorization Act and asserts that the products rely on substantial design, manufacture, assembly, or development by DJI or related entities.

(U) These clone products began appearing (notably on Amazon) in summer 2025, before DJI itself faced full Covered List restrictions. Journalists and the FCC point to near-identical hardware, software/API behavior, and interfaces as evidence they are not independent knock-offs. The move is framed as an escalation in US efforts to restrict DJI-linked technology on national-security grounds; the article notes it is the first time the FCC has used its authority to pull previously granted approvals in this context. Original DJI models from the same product generations are not yet being pulled under this specific action, though further steps remain possible.

(U) In short, US buyers lose access to these lower-cost or rebranded alternatives for new purchases, with particular impact on drone pilots seeking Mini-class aircraft and creators hoping for newer DJI-style pocket/vlogging cameras under different branding. The situation remains subject to the public comment process and any subsequent FCC decisions.
