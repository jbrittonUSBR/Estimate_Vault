(U) **Dedrone’s white paper (late 2025) argues that cheap, autonomous, and rapidly evolving uncrewed aerial systems have upended a century of air-defense assumptions.** Traditional air superiority relied on expensive crewed aircraft and pilots. Drones require no runways or crews, can launch from trucks or outside borders, and operate remotely. The paper draws on Dedrone’s 2024–2025 operational detection data from Europe, the Middle East, and Asia.

(U) Recent operations as proof points: It cites two 2025 examples. On 1 June 2025, Ukraine’s “Spider’s Web” used 117 explosive drones hidden in cargo trucks to strike nuclear-capable bombers across five Russian regions, causing an estimated $7 billion in damage at an operational cost of roughly $234,000. Twelve days later, Israel’s “Operation Rising Lion” used prepositioned small explosive drones to disrupt Iranian radar and communications, enabling over 200 fighter jets (including F-35s) to hit more than 100 targets. These illustrate how inexpensive drones can disable high-value assets and integrate with conventional strikes. Governments are responding: the EU’s €800 billion ReArm initiative through 2027 treats drones and counter-UAS as a priority, and NATO allies have raised spending targets. The paper notes emerging threats such as fiber-optically controlled (jamming-immune) drones, DIY/insect-sized platforms, and spoofing systems.

(U) Current threat picture from Dedrone data:
- Night operations: 37.5% of 2025 detections (with three months left in the year) occurred at night, suggesting deliberate exploitation of reduced visibility.
- Manufacturer concentration: DJI accounted for 83.48% of detections, Autel 1.40%, and DIY builds 9.82% (a 4.3× increase from 2024), totaling 94.70%. The remaining 5.3% is spread across many other makers. DIY growth and diversification indicate faster evolution toward harder-to-detect platforms.
- Economic imbalance: A $500 explosive FPV drone can destroy an $82.5 million F-35. Even cheap commercial drones can force costly interceptor scrambles. Attackers can mass-produce replacements far cheaper than defenders can intercept them. The concentration is an opportunity for defenders to tune sensors, playbooks, and interceptors to the dominant platforms, but a single missed drone can still cause outsized damage.

(U) Rapid evolution and expanding tactics: The paper describes a fast adaptation cycle: jamming led to pre-programmed/GPS-free flight, then inertial navigation, then fiber-optic tethers that emit no radio signals. FPV kamikaze drones and swarms followed. These capabilities are no longer limited to states—DIY kits, 3D printing, and open-source resources (e.g., Crazyswarm2 on GitHub) let non-state actors field sophisticated systems. AI (including generative models and open-source tools) accelerates design, swarm coordination, and mid-flight adaptation. Over 80% of 2025 detections used RF sensors, but RF-silent, GPS-free (SLAM, LiDAR, inertial, visual odometry), fiber-optic, spoofing, and modular-payload drones are growing. Traditional radar/RF assumptions (high/fast/large signatures) fail against low/slow/small or silent platforms. The paper argues this requires a sensor mix shift, not RF-first reliance.

(U) Future threats - Emerging categories include:
- #### Saturation/scale: “Mothership” drones (e.g., Ukraine’s GOGOL-M) releasing microdrone swarms; AI-controlled swarms of 100+; potential “mosquito clouds” of 10,000+ low-cost drones.
- #### Stealth/evasion: Radar-absorbing materials, low-heat/near-silent propulsion, RF-silent navigation, and experimental insect-sized “cybugs.”
- #### Cross-domain: Uncrewed surface/underwater vehicles and ground vehicles, plus multi-band/satellite comms.

(U) The fight becomes multi-domain (air, surface, subsurface, land) with electronic warfare, requiring integrated, networked sensors and interceptors rather than single-vector defenses.

(U) Recommended approach: Static hardware is too slow. The paper calls for software-driven, open-architecture, AI-enabled systems that adapt in real time: sensor fusion (RF + radar + acoustic + EO/IR + others), automated classification to reduce false positives, scalable/cost-matched interceptors (including attritable drones, guns, and jamming), and networked intelligence for prioritization. Dedrone positions its own AI-software, open-architecture, networked platforms as matching these needs. The conclusion stresses urgency: the next attack will likely start with a drone, and the advantage will go to whoever iterates fastest. In five years the question will be whether defenses acted soon enough.
