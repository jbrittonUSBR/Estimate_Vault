# Why Is the Coast Guard Operating Surveillance Drones in the Great Lakes?

(U) GREAT LAKES, United States — On 23 September 2026, The New York Times reported that the U.S. Coast Guard had deployed autonomous Saildrone surveillance vessels across the Great Lakes to monitor for drug trafficking and border crossings, raising privacy concerns despite limited reported illegal activity.

## Why Is the Coast Guard Operating Surveillance Drones in the Great Lakes?

#### Since May, the Coast Guard has deployed six surveillance drones across the Great Lakes to stop the flow of drugs and illegal border crossings from Canada. But there is little illegal activity on the northern border, and the sailing drones are raising privacy concerns.

“Ready to tack?” “Ready for me.” “OK.” “Send it.” Stephen and Erin Kraskowski were sailing a remote stretch of Lake Superior near the Canadian border when something caught their eye. “The whole thing looked like it was made out of carbon fiber. It’s only moving at one knot of boat speed.” “Had a little camera that was rotating.” “Its call sign is ‘No comms.’ Light bulbs start going off. Question marks start getting raised.” “The camera turned towards us and was following us.” “So somebody was watching.” This is what they saw. A 33-foot sailboat with no one on board, outfitted with a long-range camera and all sorts of sensors and radar technology. What they didn’t know was that this floating drone was part of a surveillance operation stretching across the Great Lakes and far beyond. They’re autonomous sailboat drones. The Navy and Coast Guard are using them for everything from counter-narcotics patrols in the Caribbean to wartime surveillance near the Strait of Hormuz. But six are now sailing in the largely recreational Great Lakes, which draws millions of boaters and beachgoers every year. These floating drones are arriving at a moment of deep public concern about mass surveillance, A.I., data centers and license plate readers, and as tensions grow between the U.S. and Canada. They’re part of $24 million in contracts the Coast Guard has with Saildrone, a Bay Area environmental tech company that has pivoted to defense in recent years. We wanted to see one in action, so the Coast Guard agreed to take us out onto Lake Erie, where this one’s been conducting nonstop surveillance since May. “Have you seen any illegal activity this year through the eyes of this drone?” “We haven’t had any success stories yet because we’ve learned to use the tool and build the patterns of life through these areas that we feel that it will lead to some interactions down the road.” In February 2025, the Trump administration declared drug flow across the Northern border an unusual and extraordinary threat to national security, which was the legal basis for the first round of new tariffs on Canada. But so far this year, drug seizures at the Northern border have been relatively small, around 1,000 pounds, while more than 170,000 pounds have been seized at the Southwest border. As a surveillance tool, these drones do have some limitations. They only take still photos, not video, and the Coast Guard doesn’t control the data or the drones themselves. ”Where do those pictures go? Where do they live? And if it has my photograph, where’s that going, and who’s looking at that?” ”So the information is retained by Saildrone right now. We get the feed, but we’re not retaining any of the information unless we specifically did a screenshot or something and saved it into a case file.” “How can you reassure people that they’re just not being surveilled 24/7, that their personal information and their personal privacy isn’t being infringed upon?” “We are not recording any hull numbers. We don’t have facial recognition, so we can’t tell you who is here. All we can tell you is that there is boating traffic here, and it’s going in this general direction.” Back at the station, we learned the Coast Guard doesn’t even operate the drones. Saildrone does remotely from California. “So there’s a command center in the San Francisco area. And so you’ve got a live operator, 24/7 monitoring the Saildrone.” The company told us it tracks vessels, not people, and collects no personal information, but it wouldn’t say how long it keeps the data, who sees it, or whether it’s ever sold. Those terms vary by contract. Saildrone isn’t required to worry about privacy rights. That has some civil rights groups concerned. “We see private companies increasingly at the center of law enforcement operations that have profit incentives, carrying out surveillance of what ordinary people going about their ordinary lives are doing. And I think people inherently understand that we need law enforcement in this country, but there are natural, inborn limits to how much they watch us, how much they hover over us, how much they see us, and robots and A.I. have the potential to eviscerate those limits.” For Erin and Stephen, the strangest part about all of this wasn’t the sailing drone. It’s who’s doing the watching and where that information is ultimately ending up. “If the goal is for the government to be surveilling, why isn’t the government taking charge and doing the surveillance?” “So we’re not just in a government database, we’re in some weird private company’s database.” “Growing up, you had all the privacy in the world. And the more technology we have, the less privacy you end up having.”
