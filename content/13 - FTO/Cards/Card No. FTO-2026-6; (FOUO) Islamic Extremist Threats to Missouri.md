**(U) Trends of Domestic Attacks by Islamic Extremists 

(U//FOUO) According to the U.S. intelligence community, the most likely terrorist attack scenario continues to involve U.S.-based lone attackers or small groups of radicalized young men.1 An examination of terrorist attacks by Islamic extremists from 2020 through 2025 showed a significant drop in the number of attacks, from 10 per year to an average of four per year, and the average number of fatalities per attack decreasing by 50 percent.2 Nevertheless, Islamic inspired extremism is still considered a significant threat to the U.S., second only to narco-terrorism sponsored by transnational organized crime groups.
- (U) Since 2001, most of the domestic Islamic extremist violence was carried out by U.S. citizens. 80% of domestic Islamic extremists were citizens or residents of the U.S., more than 40% were natural-born citizens.
- (U) U.S.-based attackers with no direct link to international terrorist groups are a serious and ongoing threat to the U.S. public because of the ability of Islamic groups such as Al-Qaeda to be able to inspire or direct individuals globally (i.e. the 2025 Sydney, Australia attack).

(U) Terrorism researchers claim that the relatively low number of domestic attacks by Islamic extremists in the U.S. makes the impact of any successful attack more publicly significant. Domestic terrorist groups historically had only a limited ability to execute complex attacks for a variety of reasons:
- (U) Attackers were lone operators or members of a group with only a loose affiliation between them, restricting their ability to plan, organize, purchase, or build the necessary materials for large-scale attacks.
- (U) The reliance on online radicalization to recruit attackers resulted in subjects lacking the resources, expertise or coordination for high casualty events.
- (U) The robust Federal and State-level counterterrorism measures in the U.S. enacted since 2001 have successfully disrupted or deterred large-scale attacks.
- (U) The low level of radicalization of the U.S. Muslim community, internal community vigilance, and a close cooperation with law enforcement.

**(U) Different Motives Inspire Different Attacks 

(U//FOUO) The U.S. intelligence community noted a shift in Islamic terrorists focus toward spreading propaganda to inspire or enable individuals located in the West. Islamic extremist groups interest in targeting the U.S. have focused more on virtual recruiting to encourage U.S.-based followers and enable potential attacks.9 These attackers were generally inspired by either radicalized interpretations of Islamic beliefs or a desire to punish westerners for perceived injuries against the Islamic community. This online messaging varies based on which organization develops the media content.
- (U//FOUO) Islamic State (IS) media overwhelmingly calls for attacks against soft targets, mass gatherings, and houses of worship. Its focus is to terrorize civilians and punish perceived enemies of Islam.10 IS media has been influential in inspiring domestic plots over the past decade and have highlighted the difficulty of stopping vehicle ramming attacks.
- (U//FOUO) Al-Qaeda media has been focused more on calls for targeting of U.S. military bases or Western embassies, most recently in response to grievances linked to the Israel-HAMAS conflict.13 In May 2026, Al-Qaeda released a video encouraging lone attackers to conduct “individual jihad,” consistent with their messaging inciting decentralized violence against Western targets.

(U//FOUO) Current conflicts are often a primary motivation for attackers inspired by Islamic extremist media. Al-Qaeda’s and IS’s use of social media, graphic videos and sensationist messaging has inspired a younger generation of extremists. For the past several years, teenage Islamic extremists were responsible for a significant portion of U.S.-based plots.
- (U//FOUO) Radicalization can be rapid and self-directed. Individuals often radicalize online without direct contact with terrorist groups. High-profile symbolic attacks are attractive to self radicalized individuals seeking notoriety and media attention.
- (U) Current grievances by the Islamic community in the U.S. may aggravate young men and make them more susceptible to extremist messaging. A 2026 report by a Muslim civil rights group noted some of the concerns expressed by community members in the U.S. included:
	- (U) A widening gap between constitutional guarantees and government behavior. Where government actions and official rhetoric treated Muslims with suspicion and outside the circle of protected religious and civic life.
	- (U) The perception that their religious identity was being used to disqualify them from American life. Collective guilt and punishment were used to target both non-citizens and citizens who are perceived to be part of extremist groups.

**(U) Online recruitment is the Key for Inspiring Young Men to Conduct Attacks 

(U) A review of IS-inspired attacks in 2025 by terrorism researchers showed the recruitment of young men (20 years old and younger) as a key tool for Islamic extremist groups. IS relied heavily on social media and messaging apps to fuel self-radicalization of young men, principally by focusing on support around ongoing conflicts in the Middle East and encourage lone wolf style attacks.
- (U) Within the U.S., the online environment has fueled a youth-driven mobilization. Six out of seven IS-inspired attacks or plots in the U.S. in 2025 involved at least one teenage suspect. Of the 11 actors allegedly involved, eight were teenagers at the time of the attack or when they were arrested. In five of the incidents, teenagers were the lone actors.
- (U) Iran supporters distributed videos with a strong understanding of American pop culture and featuring catchy lyrics and music across multiple social media platforms.
- (U) Researchers expect to continue to see a rise of memetic and nihilistic real-world violence in 2026, with various online subcultures increasingly responsible for lethal attacks and often perpetrated by minors.
- (U) In May 2026, a Missouri resident was linked with the IS-inspired Jan 1, 2025, attack in New Orleans, LA. The subject distributed instructional videos on social media explaining the method for making explosives. The videos were subsequently watched by the New Orleans attacker. The resulting explosive devices were found to be consistent with the same method of construction provided in the videos.

(U) Law enforcement is a national security mission, and local law enforcement is our nation’s first line of defense. 22 The detection, reporting, and immediate mitigation of threats to the homeland’s security are primarily a policing function. Local law enforcement plays a key role in the success of securing the country’s infrastructure, resources, and people. Local law enforcement's ability to interact with their communities and respond to concerns about young individuals demonstrating troubling behavior could be critical to identifying early signs of radicalization and preventing future domestic attacks. 

(U//FOUO) The MIAC assesses the primary terrorist threat to Missouri will likely continue to be domestic violent extremists supporting racially and ethnically motivated or anti-government ideologies. The MIAC makes this assessment with medium confidence based on significant incidents, disrupted plots, and intelligence community reporting. Currently, the MIAC has no information on specific or credible threats to Missouri, but the ability of these individuals to act with little to no warning, a willingness to attack civilians and soft targets, and an ability to inflict significant casualties make them the most dangerous threat to the people of Missouri.)
