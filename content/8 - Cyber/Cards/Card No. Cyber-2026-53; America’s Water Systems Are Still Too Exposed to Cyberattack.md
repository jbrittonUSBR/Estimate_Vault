(U) A recent wave of cyber intrusions targeting water and wastewater systems across at least 12 U.S. states highlights a deeper, systemic security gap in America's critical infrastructure.
### **Key Takeaways**

- **The Intrusions:** Beginning in mid-July, multiple water systems experienced cyber intrusions. Iranian-linked groups (such as the CyberAv3ngers, associated with the IRGC) claimed responsibility on Telegram, though federal agencies like the FBI and CISA have stopped short of official definitive attribution.
- **The Core Vulnerability:** Experts point out that the main issue is not just malicious actors, but why critical infrastructure remains accessible in the first place. Many water facilities rely on legacy **Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs)** and operational technology (OT) built decades ago for physical reliability—not internet connectivity. Over time, these devices have been exposed directly to the public internet or corporate IT networks without proper firewalls, VPNs, or unique credentials.
- **Regulatory Hurdles:** Efforts to mandate stricter federal cybersecurity standards have faced political and legal hurdles. A 2023 EPA rule requiring cyber reviews in water system assessments was successfully challenged by several states and ultimately withdrawn after a federal appeals court halted it.
- **The Path Forward:** Industry experts emphasize that securing operational technology requires treating OT security differently from traditional IT. Essential fixes include network segmentation, eliminating unnecessary internet exposure for industrial controls, enforcing strong identity management, and preparing manual operational workarounds.
