### Detailed Summary of the Article and Incident
The FBI concluded that a breach discovered last month (February 2026) into networks handling surveillance and wiretap data meets the threshold for a “major incident” under federal cybersecurity reporting rules (specifically the Federal Information Security Modernization Act, or FISMA, which requires notification to Congress within seven days once such a determination is made).

- **Timeline**: An inquiry into “abnormal activity” on the compromised network opened on **February 17, 2026**. The FBI first notified Congress on or around **March 4, 2026**, about suspicious activity on an internal server. The upgrade to “major incident” status occurred last week (late March/early April 2026), triggering the latest congressional alerts.
- **Affected System**: The breach targeted an **unclassified but highly sensitive internal FBI system** (sometimes referenced in related coverage as part of the bureau’s Digital Collection System Network or surveillance/wiretap management infrastructure). It contains “law enforcement sensitive information,” including:
  - Returns from legal processes such as pen registers and trap-and-trace surveillance.
  - Personally identifiable information (PII) on subjects of FBI investigations.
  - Data tied to electronic surveillance and wiretaps.
- **Scope and Location**: The intrusion affected FBI systems in the **U.S. Virgin Islands**, not FBI headquarters in Washington, D.C.
- **Response**: The incident prompted a **criminal probe** by the FBI and steps to strengthen cybersecurity across affected networks. The bureau has leveraged technical capabilities to investigate and contain it. An FBI spokesperson declined immediate comment to Bloomberg.
- **National Security Implications**: The “major incident” label signals potential risks to national security, given the sensitivity of the data involved (e.g., surveillance methods and target identities). The full extent of what may have been accessed or exfiltrated has not been publicly detailed.

### Suspected Attribution and Attack Method (Further Context)
Multiple outlets reporting on the same congressional notifications describe the breach as **China-linked**, involving sophisticated tactics. Hackers reportedly gained access by exploiting a **commercial Internet Service Provider (ISP) used as a vendor** to the FBI—essentially a supply-chain compromise rather than a direct assault on FBI systems.

This fits into a broader pattern of Chinese cyber espionage concerns. Former FBI Director Chris Wray had previously noted that the bureau opened a China-related counterintelligence case roughly every 12 hours; the threat persists under current Director Kash Patel (who visited China months earlier on fentanyl-related issues).

### Key Points (Bullet Summary)
- **Classification**: FBI/Justice Department formally deemed it a “major incident” under FISMA, requiring prompt congressional notification.
- **Discovery**: Abnormal logs flagged February 17, 2026; initial congressional briefing ~March 4.
- **Data at Risk**: Surveillance/wiretap records + PII on investigation subjects (unclassified but law-enforcement sensitive).
- **Location**: Limited to FBI systems in the Virgin Islands.
- **Attacker Profile (per other reporting)**: Suspected China-linked actors using supply-chain/ISP vendor vector.
- **FBI Actions**: Criminal investigation launched; cybersecurity enhancements underway; technical response activated.
- **Broader Context**: Builds on earlier public reports (e.g., CNN/Politico in early March) about “suspicious cyber activity” on the bureau’s surveillance data systems. It is part of ongoing U.S.-China cyber tensions, with President Trump scheduled to meet Xi Jinping next month.

No evidence has emerged of classified data exposure or widespread operational compromise, and the FBI states it has “identified and addressed” the suspicious activities. The incident highlights vulnerabilities in government supply chains and the persistent nature of nation-state cyber threats.
