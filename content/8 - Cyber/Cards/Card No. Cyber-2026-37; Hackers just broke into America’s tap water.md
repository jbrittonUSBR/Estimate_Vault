(U) Hackers, almost certainly Iranian, recently compromised water and wastewater systems in at least a dozen U.S. states, exposing how vulnerable many local utilities remain to cyber intrusion. The attacks began last week in Minnesota, where the small town of Braham and dozens of other municipalities saw their water systems knocked offline or disrupted. Effects spread to additional states and included brief service outages, boil-water notices, and some localized flooding. Systems were restored relatively quickly—Braham’s water tower was back online within hours—and there were no reported deaths, prolonged shortages, or confirmed contamination of drinking water.

(U) The article attributes the campaign to Iranian actors, citing a leaked memo, prior U.S. government warnings about Iranian targeting of American networks amid the ongoing conflict, and the pattern of disruption rather than pure financial crime. Attackers accessed internet-facing programmable logic controllers (PLCs)—the computers that run treatment plants, wells, pumps, and chemical dosing. Many of these devices were left with weak or nonexistent authentication (sometimes no password at all), no firewalls or VPNs, and public web interfaces, because they were originally designed for on-site use only. Digitization of these systems accelerated during the pandemic for remote monitoring, but cybersecurity investment lagged, especially at the roughly 97 percent of U.S. water systems that are small and locally run. Experts quoted in the piece emphasize that this is not an isolated fluke. Joshua Corman (I Am The Cavalry) notes that connectivity is expanding faster than the ability to secure it, and that nation-state actors can simply log in to exposed devices. The article recalls earlier near-misses, including a 2021 attempt in Oldsmar, Florida, to raise chemical levels dangerously high, and a Russian intrusion into a Norwegian dam. It also references reports that actors such as China have already prepositioned access in various U.S. critical infrastructure systems.

(U) While the recent incidents caused only limited, temporary disruption, the piece argues the underlying risk is serious: a more sophisticated or sustained attack could alter chemical dosing, open floodgates, or cut water entirely. Recommended mitigations include network monitoring (common in the power sector but rare for water), voluntary information-sharing (currently used by only a few hundred of the nation’s ~151,000 water facilities), greater federal and state support for small utilities, and, in some cases, simply disconnecting the most critical controls from the internet (“if you can’t protect it, disconnect it”).

(U) The article is critical of the current administration’s public response, noting the president’s denial of Iranian involvement and earlier proposed cuts to the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA). Former CISA Director Jen Easterly is cited arguing that nation-state attackers deliberately seek the weakest local points of failure. Overall, the piece frames the Minnesota-centered attacks as a warning that America’s water infrastructure remains far less protected than other everyday systems, such as credit-card networks.
