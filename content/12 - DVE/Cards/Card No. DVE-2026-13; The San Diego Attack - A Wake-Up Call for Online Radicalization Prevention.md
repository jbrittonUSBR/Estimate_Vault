)U) Just weeks ago, two teenagers opened fire at the [Islamic Center of San Diego](https://www.hstoday.us/subject-matter-areas/law-enforcement-and-public-safety/three-people-killed-in-shooting-at-san-diegos-largest-mosque-police-say/), killing three people before taking their own lives. As investigators review the attackers’ digital footprints, the factors that shaped their radicalization are coming into [clearer focus.](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/internet/extremist-online-culture-shaped-san-diego-mosque-shooters-rcna346287) The pair reportedly met online, exchanged extremist views, and drew inspiration from previous mass-casualty attacks, including the 2019 Christchurch Mosque shooting. Authorities later recovered writings expressing hatred toward multiple racial, religious, and social groups. Their worldview was not neatly confined to a single ideological movement. Rather, it reflected a convergence of grievances, dehumanization, and fascination with violence itself. For counterterrorism and prevention practitioners, the attack offers a sobering reminder that online radicalization is a complex and evolving security challenge. While terrorism is often approached through distinct ideological categories—jihadist, racially or ethnically motivated violent extremists, male supremacists, and other forms of violent extremism—the digital platforms that violent actors use increasingly [blur these boundaries.](https://www.congress.gov/116/meeting/house/109376/witnesses/HHRG-116-FA13-Wstate-RamalingamV-20190430.pdf) Today’s extremist ecosystems enable individuals to consume, remix, and reinforce narratives from disparate movements. As a result, vulnerable individuals experiencing social isolation, perceived injustice, trauma, or a lack of belonging may encounter multiple actors promoting violence as a solution —regardless of ideology. More concerning still is the emergence of violence-oriented online subcultures that are not rooted in any coherent ideological framework. Loosely configured communities such as “[764](https://eradicatehatesummit.org/wp-content/uploads/EHS-764-Infographic-Edited.pdf)” and “[The Com](https://eradicatehatesummit.org/the-com-network-law-enforcement/)” often appear motivated less by political objectives than by spectacle, status-seeking, and the gamification of violence. These ecosystems can place disaffected youth at particular risk and challenge prevention models that were designed around more traditional ideological movements. For prevention practitioners, the San Diego attack presents four important implications. First, content moderation alone is insufficient. While platform accountability remains important, removing content does not address the underlying grievances, social isolation, identity crises, and polarization that recruiters exploit. We have to get ahead of the threat. Resilience building efforts should include strengthening digital literacy, equipping individuals to recognize manipulation and disinformation, and supporting constructive outlets to tackle grievances. Second, prevention efforts must recognize that radicalization increasingly occurs across multiple online and offline environments. This is where civil society can play a vital role. Faith communities, sports clubs, schools, and other trusted local institutions are often among the first to identify changes in behavior. When equipped with appropriate training and referral pathways, these actors can help connect vulnerable individuals to behavioral health services, social support networks, and other interventions before they mobilize towards violence. Importantly, the prevention field does not need to reinvent the wheel. [Community-led prevention models](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/evaluation-multi-faceted-us-community-based-muslim-led-cve-program) have demonstrated that local partnerships among civil society organizations, faith leaders, behavioral health professionals, and law enforcement can identify risks early and provide alternatives to violence. The challenge is sustaining and modernizing these approaches for an era in which radicalization increasingly spans both online and offline spaces. Third, greater attention must be paid to youth, who are increasingly interacting with nihilistic and violence-oriented online networks. Online and offline prevention investments should prioritize digital upstander behaviors that enable early preventative interventions. These efforts can be combined with supporting youth protective factors – like access to mentors and other trusted adults that can foster a healthy sense of purpose and connection to their communities. Fourth, practitioners must better consider the gendered dimensions of radicalization. Extremist actors frequently exploit narratives related to masculinity, humiliation, protection, belonging, and gender identity. Women and girls may be targeted through technology-enabled coercion and manipulation, while young men may be drawn into ecosystems that weaponize insecurity, status anxiety, and grievance. These dynamics remain underexamined within mainstream counterterrorism frameworks despite their growing relevance. Despite the [vital role](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR2647.html) prevention efforts can play, it is important to note that this conversation is unfolding at a moment when prevention efforts have [lost funding](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/nation/2025/06/17/dp3-homeland-security-lone-attacker-program-dismantled/84235935007/) and appear to be receiving [less attention](https://substack.com/@mehreenfarooq/p-196813542) in U.S. counterterrorism policy. [The 2026 U.S. Counterterrorism Strategy](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2026-USCT-Strategy-1.pdf) acknowledges a range of threats, including those enabled by digital technologies, but places considerably greater emphasis on disruption, law enforcement, border security, and military tools than on community-based prevention, resilience-building, or early intervention efforts. If the public sector cannot sustain long-term prevention investments, other private and social sector actors will need to step forward. Philanthropies, technology funders, and democracy-focused donors have invested heavily in information integrity, civic engagement, and digital literacy. Increasingly, they should view preventing online radicalization and violence as part of that same mission. Given [the speed and scale of radicalization](https://substack.com/@mehreenfarooq/p-197376634) that digital platforms now provide, prevention cannot remain an afterthought. Kinetic-heavy counter-terrorism responses may disrupt plots and networks, but they do little to address the underlying conditions that make individuals vulnerable to recruitment in the first place. The challenge is not that we lack prevention models—it is whether we are willing to invest in and adapt them to meet today’s evolving threat environment.
