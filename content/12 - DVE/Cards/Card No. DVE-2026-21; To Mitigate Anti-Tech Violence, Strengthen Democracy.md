(U) It argues that a purely national-security-focused response to rising anti-tech violence is insufficient, and that policymakers instead need to address the underlying legitimacy of public grievances about AI.

**Main argument and evidence cited:**

(U) The piece opens with three incidents it treats as evidence of a growing trend: an April 6 shooting in which a man fired 13 shots at the home of Indianapolis Councilman Ron Gibson, who had backed a data center project, with a note reading "NO DATA CENTERS" left at the scene; an alleged Molotov cocktail attack on OpenAI CEO Sam Altman's house by Daniel Moreno-Gama, who was later arrested attempting to break into OpenAI headquarters and was found with a manifesto containing a kill list of AI leaders; and an FBI-disrupted drone-explosive plot targeting a planned UFC fight event at the White House, tied to a suspect in contact with an "ultra-religious and anti-government" group concerned about data centers' water use. The author situates this within a broader thesis (citing researcher Mauro Lubrano and terrorism scholar Yannick Veilleux-Lepage) that anti-technology extremism is emerging as a distinct ideological current cutting across the political spectrum — from far-right ecofascism to far-left environmentalist sabotage groups like Germany's "Volcano Group" — driven by economic disruption, institutional distrust, and an "accountability gap" around AI harms. The piece then argues that blurred lines between tech elites and the Trump administration (citing DOGE, Musk's role, tech CEO access to Trump, and rising lobbying spending) have eroded public trust, citing survey figures including that only 17 percent of Americans in 2025 said they trusted the government to do the right thing "just about always" or "most of the time," and that only 15 percent of Americans said they trust AI companies to make decisions about how AI is developed and used, per an Anthropic Public Record survey released in June 2026. The author concludes that policymakers should address legitimate grievances (e.g., state-level data center moratoriums) rather than relying solely on expanded surveillance, which the piece argues could itself validate anti-tech sentiment and risk political repression.
