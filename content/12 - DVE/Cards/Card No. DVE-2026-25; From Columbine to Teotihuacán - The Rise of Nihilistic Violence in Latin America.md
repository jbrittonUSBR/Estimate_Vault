(U) **Core argument:** The piece argues that the April 20, 2026 shooting at Mexico's Teotihuacán pyramids — where 27-year-old Julio César Jasso Ramírez killed one tourist and injured thirteen before killing himself — is not an isolated incident but part of a broader, previously underexplored pattern of "nihilistic violence" spreading across Latin America, linked to the online "True Crime Community" (TCC) milieu that glorifies mass-violence perpetrators.

**Teotihuacán attack details:** The author reports the attacker's alleged (since-inactive) X account contained Nazi/fascist imagery, including a photo of him performing a Nazi salute and content glorifying Mussolini and Francoism, plus a false claim of being based in Seville. During the attack he delivered remarks in peninsular Spanish and referenced the site's pre-Hispanic sacrificial symbolism. The attack coincided with the Columbine anniversary/Hitler's birthday (April 20), and he reportedly possessed an AI-generated image of himself with Columbine perpetrators Eric Harris and Dylan Klebold, and had left a pre-attack YouTube comment naming Columbine and declaring himself "the protagonist in Teotihuacán." Content glorifying him ("rampage edits") reportedly circulated on TikTok afterward, in apparent violation of platform policy. The piece notes Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum and prosecutors framed the attack as tied to the perpetrator's psychological condition, while the author argues it fits a transnational pattern.

**Broader regional pattern:** The article traces earlier Mexican precedents (2017 Colegio Americano de Monterrey, 2020 Colegio Cervantes/Torreón) and at least four more recent cases since March 2024 — University of Technology of Guadalajara (March 2024, triple femicide), San Andrés preparatory school (November 2024, Nazi symbolism, livestreamed), UNAM stabbing (September 2025), Lázaro Cárdenas shooting (March 2026) — plus incidents in Chile (Calama, March 2026), Argentina (Córdoba school shooting and a foiled plot, March 2026), and a cross-border "tomorrow, shooting" viral threat wave in Argentina, Chile, Peru, and Mexico linked to a TikTok trend. It identifies Brazil's 2019 Suzano massacre as a major regional reference point cited by other Latin American attackers.

**Framing/caveats the author herself includes:** She frames "nihilistic violence" as violence lacking clear ideological motivation, driven instead by misanthropic worldviews and a desire for symbolic status/notoriety within online communities — citing related concepts like "participatory memetic violent extremism." She cites UNICEF-sourced figures that roughly one in four Latin American adolescents experience school bullying and about 15% face mental health problems, framed as contextual (not causal) risk factors. She recommends stronger platform enforcement against glorification content and cross-platform/regional coordination.
