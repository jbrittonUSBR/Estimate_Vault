(U) In the early hours a few weeks ago in Baghdad, Mohammad al-Saadi and his colleagues got to work. From their darkened lair in the Iraqi capital the men allegedly used Apple’s FaceTime app to connect with an individual who was about to firebomb a synagogue more than 3,000 miles away in north London. While al-Saadi recorded proceedings in real time, another man in the Baghdad bunker ordered the person in the UK to take a petrol bomb and “light one in your hand”, according to US court papers. “Light it,” the man in Iraq added in English. “Throw the fourth one.” The arson attack on London’s Jewish community in April, in which no one was injured, is one of at least 18 across Europe that US prosecutors claim were masterminded by al-Saadi, 33, at the behest of Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC). The attacks, including others allegedly planned for North America, are said to be part of a campaign of “psychological warfare” using criminal proxies recruited online to retaliate against the US-Israeli military strikes on Iran that began on February 28. Al-Saadi’s capture last month while he was passing through Istanbul, and his subsequent handover to the FBI, has been hailed as one of the most significant counter-terrorism arrests in recent years. [Kash Patel](https://www.thetimes.com/us/american-politics/article/kash-patel-fbi-bourbon-free-alchohol-r6dgxh753), the US bureau’s director, has described the dual Iranian-Iraqi national as a “high-value target responsible for mass global terrorism” — an assessment shared by British security officials. On the flight from Turkey to New York, where he will eventually stand trial, al-Saadi allegedly revealed to the FBI that he had met [Ayatollah Ali Khamenei](https://www.thetimes.com/topic/ayatollah-ali-khamenei) in Tehran three days before Iran’s supreme leader was killed at the start of the war with the US and Israel. He also claimed that he was “like a son” to [Qasem Soleimani](https://www.thetimes.com/uk/obituaries/article/general-qasem-soleimani-obituary-hb8g7ng3c), the former head of the IRGC’s Quds Force, an elite unit responsible for overseas operations, according to a 35-page court indictment obtained by The Sunday Times. Al-Saadi, charged with eight terrorism-related offences in the US, could spend the rest of his life in prison if convicted. Prosecutors in New York say they will seek evidence from the phones of some suspects in the UK and Europe as part of their case. When al-Saadi appeared at a preliminary court hearing in Manhattan this month, he entered a not guilty plea and suggested that all is fair at a time of conflict. “I’m not guilty and in a war situation,” he told the district judge Colleen McMahon through an interpreter. “I’m a prisoner of war. I’m not a threat. Our children and women are being killed by your rockets.” So how did a man who most people had never heard of before end up as one of the FBI’s most wanted targets? Born Mohammad Baqer Saad Dawood al-Saadi in Tehran in 1993, his Shia Muslim parents had moved from their native Iraq when the country was ruled by Saddam Hussein and his Sunni minority-dominated Ba’ath Party. His father, Saad Dawood, is thought to have been a fighter at one time in the Badr Brigades, an IRGC-backed Shia militia opposed to Saddam’s rule. Analysts believe that the family, including his two siblings, returned to Baghdad after the US-led invasion and the fall of the Iraqi dictator in 2003. Al-Saadi appears to have followed in his father’s footsteps by later joining another Iraqi militia, Kata’ib Hezbollah, loyal to Tehran and fiercely defiant of the US occupation. It has been designated by Washington as a terrorist organisation. During his rapid rise through the ranks, al-Saadi is said to have carried out stints, as a 20-year-old, fighting alongside Bashar al-Assad’s army in Syria, and later against the Islamic State in Iraq. By the time he was a commander in Kata’ib Hezbollah, he had struck up a close relationship with Soleimani, the IRGC’s most lauded general, who was considered to be Khamenei’s right-hand man. “This arrest is very significant given both al-Saadi’s close eyes to figures like Qasem Soleimani and Esmail Qaani [Soleimani’s successor in the IRGC],” said Dr Matthew Levitt, a former FBI official and director of counter-terrorism and intelligence at the Washington Institute for Near East Policy. “That someone this young was allegedly entrusted with overseeing such a sensitive operation says a lot about his stature, not only within Iraqi Shia militias but also within the Quds Force.” According to the indictment in New York, al-Saadi was supposed to be driving [Soleimani](https://www.thetimes.com/uk/obituaries/article/general-qasem-soleimani-obituary-hb8g7ng3c), 62, to meet the leader of Kata’ib Hezbollah, Abu Mahdi al-Muhandis, when the two senior figures were killed by a US airstrike at Baghdad airport in January 2020. The double assassination left al-Saadi devastated, and within months he was posting threats against America on his Twitter account. One post featured an image of Capitol Hill in ruins alongside the caption: “Our revenge for the martyred leaders is ongoing.” The threats are likely to have placed al-Saadi in the sights of US intelligence agencies if he was not already on their radar.
## From Russia with arms

(U) Messages and videos discovered on al-Saadi’s iPhone, and on his social media accounts — many of which have been seen by The Sunday Times — have separately proved to be a treasure trove of information for investigators, including evidence of his travels across Europe. In the early 2020s he apparently became involved in drone procurement efforts for Iraqi militia groups and travelled to several European countries. Al-Saadi is not known to have ever visited the UK. Immigration stamps in al-Saadi’s Iraqi passport — which lists his occupation as a “government official” — indicate he spent a fortnight in Spain in August 2023. His Schengen area visa was later revoked by the Italian authorities, although the reason for the cancellation is unclear. In July 2024, al-Saadi attended an arms fair in Moscow and was photographed in Red Square, based on images discovered on his Telegram account. He returned to the Russian capital for a similar event the next summer and posed for photographs at the Iranian government’s official stand. Moscow is a key ally of Tehran, and so al-Saadi’s trips to Russia may raise questions about what the Kremlin and GRU military intelligence agency knew about his activities. While al-Saadi may have been legitimately working in defence procurement, a new research paper by Crispin Smith and Michael Knights for the Combating Terrorism Center at the US Military Academy, West Point, New York, indicates that his travels may have afforded him other opportunities. “[It is] likely that at this time al-Saadi began to develop contacts in both the Iranian and Iraqi diasporas in Europe, as well as in regional criminal organisations,” they write. The hiring of gangsters and impressionable youths by Iran to carry out its dirty work abroad has become an increasingly common phenomenon in the UK and elsewhere. His Snapchat profile picture shows him standing in front of the Eiffel Tower in Paris, although the date of his trip to France is not known.

## The war ‘solution’

(U) Al-Saadi told FBI interrogators last month that he was in Tehran to meet Khamenei, 86, about three days before the ayatollah was [killed by an Israeli airstrike](https://www.thetimes.com/uk/obituaries/article/ayatollah-ali-khamenei-obituary-death-jqkz35szd) on February 28. He is subsequently alleged to have been involved in “high-level discussions” with IRGC leaders, including Qaani, about a “solution” to the military conflict with the West. US prosecutors suggest that a plan to set up an arms-length Islamist front group, Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamiya (Hayi), appears to have been hatched in advance of a wave of terror attacks against Iran’s enemies across Europe. A copy of a founding “charter” for Hayi was found on al-Saadi’s phone when he was captured. The document included the following statement, translated from Arabic: “From today, we declare clearly — the United States, the Israeli Zionist regime and anyone who co-operates with them at any level will not be secure.” On March 7, al-Saadi shared an ominous post on the encrypted Telegram app addressed to “shadow soldiers”. The message gave “permission to all silent cells” to get ready “for work”, according to the US indictment. Two days later, an explosion took place outside the entrance to a synagogue in the Belgian city of Liege. The incident was followed by attacks on consecutive days on a synagogue in the Dutch port of Rotterdam, and a Jewish school and an American bank, both in Amsterdam. Soon afterwards there was a firebombing attack in Golders Green, northwest London. It was the first of at least half a dozen incidents in the capital over the next few weeks, most of them targeting Jews. Other arson attacks took place in Paris, Munich and Skopje, North Macedonia. No fatalities or injuries were recorded, but the wave of rudimentary strikes left communities terrified. Hayi, which translates from Arabic as the Islamic Movement of the Companions of the Right, claimed responsibility for the incidents in a series of slick propaganda videos circulated on social media. Many featured raw footage from the scene of an attack. As the campaign escalated, MI5 and counter-terrorism police at Scotland Yard compared notes with their European counterparts and concluded that Hayi was merely an online entity and likely to be a front, or “cut-out”, for Iran and its allies. The evidence appeared to be pointing towards IRGC-backed militias in Iraq. On April 29, Hayi also sought to claim credit for the stabbing of two Jewish men in Golders Green, although UK officials believe this was an “opportunistic” move. A 45-year-old man has been charged with attempted murder. On the same day, al-Saadi released a staged or AI-generated image on Telegram purporting to show Hayi operatives poring over a map of Europe in a military bunker. 

## The net closes in

(U) The Metropolitan Police shared information about the London firebombings with the FBI. But the US agency seems to have had an ace up its sleeve that would ultimately ensnare al-Saadi. Eager to replicate the success of the antisemitic attacks in Europe, al-Saadi allegedly tried to recruit a member of a Mexican drugs cartel in April to target Jews in the US. He offered to pay the gang member $10,000 in crypto-currency for attacks on three sites, including a synagogue in Manhattan, according to the US indictment. In reality, however, al-Saadi was talking to an undercover FBI agent. On April 30, things seemed to escalate with al-Saadi allegedly sounding out the would-be recruit about the possible “killing” of Jews as part of any attack. The next day, al-Saadi was detained by armed Turkish police at an Istanbul hotel. He is thought to have been in transit to a European country at the time of his arrest. Andrew Dalack, al-Saadi’s defence lawyer in the US, suspects his client had been under surveillance for some time before his capture. He said al-Saadi was held in an “underground Turkish prison” for a fortnight before being handed to the FBI on May 14. Dalack wants to know if his client was mistreated at any point in Turkey and could have made a series of confessions to the FBI under duress when he was flown to New York. “The circumstances surrounding his treatment are of substantial interest to me because they will inform arguments that I may be able to make about the admissibility of the post-arrest statements that [al-Saadi] made to the FBI,” Dalack said. Al-Saadi is now being held in solitary confinement at the Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn and has not been able to speak to his mother and siblings for about six weeks. The prison’s other inmates include [Nicolás Maduro](https://www.thetimes.com/us/american-politics/article/nothing-off-the-table-as-trump-weighs-military-response-in-cuba-tpz90jlq7), the deposed Venezuelan president accused by the US of cocaine trafficking and corruption. US prosecutors want to obtain evidence gathered from the phones of some suspects in the London arson attacks to help them build their case for al-Saadi’s trial, which is likely to happen next year. Sarah Kushner, an assistant US attorney, told a court hearing on June 1: “A large part of this case relates to 18 attacks that occurred in Europe. Individuals have been arrested in Europe in connection with those attacks and devices have been recovered from at least some of those individuals that the [US] government is working on obtaining copies.” Counter Terrorism Policing London said: “Officers are liaising with relevant authorities in the US in relation to the individual facing charges in New York.” Al-Saadi may be in US custody after the recent spate of antisemitic firebombings. However, security officials believe it’s only a matter of time before Tehran attempts its next proxy attack on Britain.
