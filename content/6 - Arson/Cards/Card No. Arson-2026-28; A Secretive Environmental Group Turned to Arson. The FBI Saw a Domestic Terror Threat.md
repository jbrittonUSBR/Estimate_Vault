(U) In his latest work, *Fires in the Night: The Earth Liberation Front, the FBI, and a Secret History of Eco-Sabotage*, author Matthew Wolfe explores the controversial actions of the Earth Liberation Front (ELF), an extremist environmental group that engaged in arson across the United States during the 1990s and early 2000s. Wolfe’s narrative focuses on a pivotal incident in 1998, when a fire at the Two Elk Lodge in Vail, Colorado, caused $15 million in damages and propelled the ELF to the forefront of the FBI’s domestic terrorism concerns. Despite the FBI flagging the ELF as a primary threat, it is noteworthy that no lives were lost in the group’s series of incendiary attacks, which included targets like logging companies and luxury car dealerships. Wolfe’s exploration of their motives raises complex ethical questions about the desperation of environmental activism when traditional means of protest seem ineffective. The author paints a period marked by a peculiar mix of optimism and despair. The 1990s, for many, represented prosperity and the potential for real change. Yet, this was simultaneously overshadowed by an increasing awareness of ecological degradation and a pervasive sense of urgency among activists. Wolfe argues that such disillusionment motivated members of the ELF to adopt drastic measures, including arson, as they sought to draw attention to what they perceived as imminent environmental collapse. Wolfe presents a compelling argument for understanding the ELF’s actions within the context of the time. While acknowledging the moral ambiguity inherent in their tactics, he also highlights the federal government’s inflated perception of the group as a terrorist entity. In his view, the FBI’s classification was coloured by the rising anxieties following the September 11 attacks, which led them to allocate substantial resources toward investigating environmental activists like the ELF. A significant theme in Wolfe’s writing emerges from his reflections on both the activists and the law enforcement agents pursuing them. He surprises readers with the discovery of how personal backgrounds defined their actions. For example, Kevin Tubbs, one of the ELF’s co-founders, was raised with aspirations of joining the Marines, which he combined with a passion for punk music, ultimately guiding his radical path. Conversely, FBI Agent John Ferreira’s relentless commitment to capturing the ELF reflected the same level of passion, underscoring how individuals’ identities shaped the broader narrative. As Wolfe delves deeper into the motivations of ELF members, he notes their belief that conventional activism was failing to address the ongoing ecological crises. They saw their actions as a necessary rebellion against a system they deemed dysfunctional and unresponsive. This sentiment points to a broader question about the efficacy and morality of civil disobedience in the face of dire predictions regarding the planet’s future. When asked whether he views the ELF’s story primarily as a crime narrative, an environmental tale, or a reflection on failing institutions, Wolfe suggests that it encapsulates all three dimensions. He posits that the saga of the ELF unfurls against a backdrop of passionate individuals resorting to illegality to highlight the shortcomings of established systems expected to safeguard against environmental degradation. Wolfe’s work becomes particularly relevant in today’s climate discourse, as public concern for the environment continues to intensify. The lessons drawn from the operations of the ELF resonate with contemporary movements advocating for climate action, posing the question: what recourse is left when all other avenues of protest appear closed? In his exploration, Wolfe captures the psychological and moral complexities of taking a stand in a world under threat. Readers may find themselves grappling with the implications of Wolfe’s narrative, reflecting on the choices facing activists today and the challenges that accompany the fight for ecological justice. *Fires in the Night*, now available in bookstores, invites readers to confront the intricacies of activism and the dire straits of environmental advocacy in the face of a changing planet. With an unflinching look at both sides—those who break the law to effect change and those who seek to uphold it—Wolfe fosters a dialogue that remains pressing in today’s fragile environmental landscape.
