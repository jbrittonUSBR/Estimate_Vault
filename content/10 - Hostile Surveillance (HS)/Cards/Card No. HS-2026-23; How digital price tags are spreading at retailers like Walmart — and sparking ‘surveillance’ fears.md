(U) Walmart and other major retailers are rapidly expanding the use of digital electronic shelf tags to replace traditional paper price labels, promising faster updates and operational efficiencies, but the rollout is sparking significant backlash over potential surveillance capabilities and dynamic pricing risks. Walmart plans to install the LED-based tags across all 4,600 of its U.S. stores by the end of 2026, reducing the time needed for price changes from days to minutes, while competitors like Kohl’s and PC Richard & Son have already adopted similar systems for years. Labor unions and privacy advocates warn that the tags—some equipped with Bluetooth sensors—could enable real-time price manipulation, facial recognition, or tracking of shoppers and workers, potentially leading to price gouging and job losses, prompting legislative efforts in Congress and several states to regulate or ban the technology. Retailers counter that the devices are primarily efficiency tools with no current use for dynamic in-store pricing and that fears are overstated, though critics point to past experiments by companies like Instacart and the broader trend of surveillance capitalism as reasons for concern. The debate highlights growing tensions between retail innovation and consumer/worker protections amid advancing retail technology.
