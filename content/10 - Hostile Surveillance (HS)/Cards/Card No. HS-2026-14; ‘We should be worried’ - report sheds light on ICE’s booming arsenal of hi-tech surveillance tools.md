(U) A new report by immigrant rights and surveillance watchdog groups reveals that U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and Customs and Border Protection (CBP) have dramatically expanded their use of advanced surveillance technologies, with spending on contracts for AI-powered tools, facial recognition, drones, data brokers, and other monitoring systems surging from under $50 million in 2013 to a record $513 million in 2026. The report, drawing on federal contracting data, highlights major players like Palantir and Anduril, and details how the Department of Homeland Security has funneled hundreds of millions through programs like the Small Business Innovation Research initiative to develop invasive capabilities such as device data extraction, AI-generated dossiers on migrants and activists, and real-time tracking systems. Critics, including report co-author Paromita Shah, warn of insufficient oversight, potential civil rights violations, and the risk of a “dystopian surveillance state” where technology enables warrantless monitoring and erodes privacy, especially as ICE’s budget makes it the best-funded law enforcement agency in the country. While ICE and Palantir have denied maintaining databases on U.S. citizen protesters or engaging in surveillance themselves, the report underscores how these tools—accelerated under the current administration—are reshaping immigration enforcement and raising urgent questions about accountability and the future of civil liberties.
