(U) **AI and Warrantless Foreign Intelligence Surveillance** (Just Security, July 22, 2026)

(U) This analysis examines how large language models (LLMs) amplify risks in two major U.S. foreign intelligence surveillance authorities that collect vast amounts of Americans’ communications without individualized warrants: FISA Section 702 and Executive Order 12333.

**Section 702**:
- Authorizes targeting of specific foreign persons/entities overseas; “incidentally” collects Americans’ communications with those targets. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court (FISC) approves procedures but does not approve individual targets.
- The program lapsed in June 2026 but continues under prior FISC approvals while reauthorization is debated.
- The NSA refuses to quantify the volume of U.S.-person communications collected. The FBI has queried 702 databases millions of times for U.S. persons, with the FISC finding “persistent and widespread” violations (including searches related to protesters and political donors).
- LLMs could expand the pool of foreign targets (already near 350,000 in 2025), increasing incidental collection of Americans’ data. They can synthesize large volumes of communications and interpret nuanced language, raising civil-liberties risks that existing querying rules and minimization procedures may not adequately constrain.

**Executive Order 12333**:
- Broader than Section 702: permits bulk collection overseas without specific targets or selectors and without any judicial supervision or routine transparency reporting.
- Collection can include entire datasets (e.g., all calls into/out of a country or data from foreign data centers). Historical examples include the NSA’s SOMALGET program (recording nearly all mobile calls in the Bahamas for a rolling 30-day window) and CIA bulk collection of financial records and contacts with foreigners.
- Volume is believed to far exceed Section 702. Minimization rules (retention limits, masking of U.S.-person identities, access controls, purpose limits) are weaker than under 702, vary by agency, and are entirely executive-branch supervised.

**How LLMs weaken existing safeguards**:
- **Retention**: Models can process vast volumes and generate foreign-intelligence justifications at scale, making it easier to retain data beyond the usual five-year limit.
- **Masking**: Identity-masking protections (replacing names with generic designators and requiring formal unmasking requests) are undermined when models can infer identities or relationships from context and training data.
- Overall, rules designed for human-scale review and earlier analytic tools become less effective against AI systems that can analyze, connect, and interpret data far more comprehensively.

(U) The author argues that applying LLMs to these warrantless collections expands government capabilities against Americans’ data, erodes procedural constraints, and risks producing less reliable intelligence, all while remaining largely outside robust judicial or public oversight.
