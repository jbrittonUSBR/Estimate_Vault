(U) Recent radiological incidents in Europe, including a highly radioactive banknote intercepted at Poland’s Medyka border crossing in April 2026 and a pro-Iranian group’s claimed drone attack with radioactive material near the Israeli embassy in London, underscore that small-scale nuclear and radiological threats are no longer hypothetical and expose critical gaps in Europe’s detection capabilities. Designed primarily for large-scale atmospheric events like Chornobyl, Europe’s systems are ill-suited to sub-threshold dangers involving contaminated items or drones transported through civilian logistics networks, as seen in past state-linked assassinations. While the US provides some border and urban radiation detection infrastructure, it is increasingly shifting primary responsibility for CBRN protection to European allies, even as Iran maintains large unaccounted stocks of highly enriched uranium. The EU has developed strategies and a strategic reserve through rescEU but lacks a comprehensive, proactive detection framework capable of early warning against dispersed, low-level threats. Authors argue that Europe must prioritize dedicated investment in modern detection infrastructure during the upcoming 2028–2034 Multiannual Financial Framework negotiations, leveraging existing institutions and commercial capabilities to build a resilient system rather than remaining reactive or dependent on external partners.
