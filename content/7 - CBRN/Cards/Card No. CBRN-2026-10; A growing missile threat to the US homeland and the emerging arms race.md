(U) Recent US intelligence assessments, including the 2026 Annual Threat Assessment and the May 2025 Defense Intelligence Agency report commissioned for the “Golden Dome” missile-defense initiative, project a more than fivefold increase in missile threats to the US homeland—from roughly 3,000 to over 16,000 by 2035—driven by expansion in advanced long-range systems and cheaper, expendable weapons such as one-way attack UAVs from China, Russia, Iran, North Korea, and Pakistan. China’s ICBMs are projected to grow from 400 to 700 and its SLBMs from 72 to at least 132, while boosted hypersonic weapons and land-attack cruise missiles are expected to see the largest increases (China’s hypersonics rising from 600 to 4,000 and LACMs from 1,000 to 5,000; Russia showing comparable growth). These developments, accelerated by extensive missile use in the Ukraine and Middle East conflicts, have prompted the US to ramp up production of low-cost cruise missiles, aero-ballistic systems, and missile-defense interceptors while adversaries respond to US strategic missile-defense efforts by expanding their own arsenals. The assessments suggest the emergence of a conventional arms race centered on sheer numbers of systems—potentially overshadowing any nuclear one—amid budget constraints on warhead production, heightened vulnerability to long-range strikes, and the risk that layered missile defenses and offensive buildups will fuel further escalation among major powers.
