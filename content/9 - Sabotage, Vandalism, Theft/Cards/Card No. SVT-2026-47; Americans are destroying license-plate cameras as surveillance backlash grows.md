(U) A wave of public backlash against automated license plate readers (ALPRs)—particularly those manufactured by the surveillance technology company Flock Safety—has sparked widespread protests, contract cancellations, and a surge in physical vandalism across the United States.
### **Key Takeaways**

- **The Scale of Surveillance:** Flock Safety cameras are utilized by over 5,000 law enforcement agencies and capture billions of vehicle records monthly. The technology maps out driver movements over time, tracking daily commutes, medical visits, and school drop-offs.
- **Fuel for the Backlash:** Public anger intensified following investigative reporting (including from _The Washington Post_) that uncovered numerous instances of police misconduct, such as officers abusing the database to stalk ex-partners or track citizens without proper warrants. Critics and privacy groups like the ACLU argue that mass data collection violates Fourth Amendment protections against unreasonable searches.
- **Vandalism and Resistance:** Frustrated by a lack of political responsiveness, some citizens have turned to direct action. Flock cameras have been vandalized, sawed down, shot at, covered in paint, or physically blocked across dozens of states. Meanwhile, grassroots groups like _DeFlock_ map the cameras online, helping push at least 100 cities to deactivate or cancel their surveillance contracts.
- **Industry Response:** In the wake of intense scrutiny, Flock announced new software safeguards, such as requiring police to link searches to specific criminal case numbers, automatically flagging abnormal search behavior, and shortening default data retention periods. However, tensions between public privacy advocates, law enforcement utility, and direct civil resistance remain high.
