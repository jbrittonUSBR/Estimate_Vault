(U) **Teenage saboteurs. 240 minors were accused of sabotage in Russia since the start of the full-scale war against Ukraine** (Mediazona, Olga Romashova, July 21, 2026) Since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, at least 240 minors have been detained on sabotage or terrorism charges related to arson attacks, according to Mediazona’s count (with assistance from OVD-Info and Avtozak LIVE). In the vast majority of cases (205), investigators attributed the motive to a desire to earn money; six acted under the influence of scammers, five expressed opposition to the war, and the rest had unclear motives.

**How recruitment works**:
(U) Teenagers looking for easy side jobs online (often as “sportiks”—athletic people willing to perform minor criminal tasks for pay) encounter anonymous Telegram channels and darknet forums offering money to set fires. Clients typically frame the jobs as insurance fraud or other “shady schemes,” downplaying the legal risks. Common targets are railway relay cabinets, cell towers, transformers, locomotives, and sometimes military equipment (helicopters, fighter jets). Perpetrators are usually required to film the act and make specific hand gestures. Russian authorities frequently attribute the clients to Ukrainian intelligence; some videos have appeared on Ukraine’s Main Intelligence Directorate (HUR) channels. Human rights groups note that the FSB has also conducted provocations to identify willing participants. Examples include 15- and 16-year-old school students who set fire to a locomotive cab in 2024 (receiving 9 and 6 years in prison) and larger groups that progressed from relay cabinets to attempts on military aircraft. Promised payments range from roughly 10,000 rubles (~$130) upward, but many are cheated and receive nothing—especially for higher-value targets.

**Legal consequences**:
(U) Arson is charged as a terrorist act (Article 205) or sabotage (Article 281). Criminal liability begins at age 14 (recently lowered for sabotage from 16). Some face additional treason charges if correspondence is deemed contact with Ukrainian services. Sentences for teenagers often start at three years in a juvenile facility; the longest known terms reach nine years in a penal colony. The youngest detained was 11 (below the age of responsibility). Authorities also use public warnings, including stencils on relay cabinets threatening life imprisonment and police messaging that “arson isn’t a trend—it’s a crime.” The article details peer influence, injuries suffered during attacks, and the broader pattern of young people being drawn into high-risk acts for small sums of money.
