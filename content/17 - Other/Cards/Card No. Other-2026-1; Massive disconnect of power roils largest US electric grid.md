(U) **Massive disconnect of power roils largest US electric grid** (Reuters, Tim McLaughlin, July 22, 2026) A large amount of data-center power abruptly disconnected from the PJM Interconnection grid—the largest U.S. electric grid—on Wednesday, causing a voltage disturbance felt from Washington, D.C., to Chicago.

**Key details**:
- The epicenter was northern Virginia, home to the world’s largest concentration of data centers.
- When an area transmission line went out of service Wednesday morning, data centers’ control systems automatically unplugged from the grid and switched to backup power, according to Dominion Energy (the regional utility).
- PJM reported more than 3 gigawatts of power demand going offline—about 3% of the grid’s total electric demand at the time.
- PJM registered the sudden drop in demand and a change in grid frequency but said there were no impacts to the grid’s reliability.
- Dominion said its system operations team stabilized the situation and returned to normal operating conditions within minutes.
- Independent monitoring (Ting Labs, with 1.4 million sensors) recorded voltage disturbances extending along the eastern United States to Chicago. The event took about 10 minutes to fully stabilize—far longer than the milliseconds typical for routine corrections. Customers in northern Virginia reported flickering lights and noises from air conditioners and refrigerators.

"Within minutes of the incident occurring, our system operations team was able to stabilize the situation and return to normal operating conditions," Dominion said. The Virginia event underscores why federal and state regulators ​are studying measures to better manage sudden drops in demand from data centers and ​crypto miners that can destabilize grids. Bob Marshall, CEO of Ting Labs, said his company's network of 1.4 million sensors recorded ‌a ⁠range of voltage disturbances that extended along the eastern United States and to Chicago in the Midwest. Grid disturbances are routinely corrected by operators and utilities in a matter of milliseconds, Marshall said. But Wednesday's event took about 10 minutes before the grid was fully stabilized, ​he said. "This was a ​massive event," Marshall said. "Our ⁠customers in northern Virginia reported flickering lights and noises coming from their air conditioners and refrigerators. The power quality took a pretty ​big hit." PJM Interconnection, the grid operator for 67 million people in ​a territory that ⁠stretches from Washington to Chicago, reported more than 3 gigawatts of power demand going offline, or about 3% of the grid's total electric demand at the time, according to PJM operations ⁠data. PJM said ​it registered the sudden drop in power demand and ​a change in the grid's frequency, which measures the grid's balance of electricity supply and demand.
